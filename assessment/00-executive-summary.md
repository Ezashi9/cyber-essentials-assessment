# Executive summary

**To:** Managing Partner, Quillmere Accountancy Ltd (a fictional firm)
**About:** Cyber security risk assessment, October 2026

## The short version

Quillmere holds bank statements, tax records, National Insurance numbers and passport scans for about 600 clients. That makes it worth a criminal's time. Today, a single stolen password would let someone read years of client email and send convincing emails in the firm's name.

The good news: the most important fixes are already included in what you pay for. They just aren't switched on. About two weeks of effort and under £2,500 would remove the worst of the risk.

## Top 5 risks

| | Risk | In plain English | Today |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Email account takeover | Someone clicks a fake login link. With only a password protecting email, the criminal is straight in | Critical |
| 2 | Payment fraud | A fake email tells a client, or us, to pay into a "new" bank account. Nobody phones to check | High |
| 3 | Weak and reused passwords | Staff reuse personal passwords. Work passwords sit in a spreadsheet and a notebook | High |
| 4 | Everyone has full control of their laptop | If anyone opens a bad attachment, it can do anything to that laptop and spread from there | High |
| 5 | Ransomware with no safe backup | Laptops miss updates, and the only backup disk is always plugged in, so it would be locked too | High |

Ten more risks are in the full register. In total: 1 Critical, 11 High and 3 Medium.

## What to do first (next 2 weeks)

1. **Turn on two-step sign-in** (a code from a phone app as well as a password) for email and the accounting and payroll software. A stolen password is then no longer enough on its own.
2. **Phone to check any new bank details**, using a number already on file, before any money moves.
3. **Close old accounts and shared logins**: one former employee can still sign in.
4. **Stop using the old reception PC** for email and the web. It no longer gets security fixes. Order a replacement.
5. **Give staff a password manager** and delete the password spreadsheet.

Cost: about £1,100 to £2,400, mostly the new PC. Most items are free.

## Next 3 months

Upgrade the Microsoft 365 plan so laptops and phones can be managed from one place, take everyday admin rights away, set up a backup that ransomware can't reach, and write a one-page plan for what to do if something goes wrong. Then apply for **Cyber Essentials**, the government-backed certificate that proves these basics are in place.

Cost: about £2,700 to £4,300 in the first year, then roughly £1,800 to £3,400 a year for everything.

## The business case

- **It's common.** The government's [Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026) found 46% of small businesses had a cyber breach or attack in the last year. Phishing was by far the most common kind.
- **One incident costs more than the fix.** A single diverted client payment, a week of lost work in January, or writing to 600 clients about a data breach would each cost more than the whole first-year plan.
- **It's a legal duty.** Under UK GDPR, the firm must protect client data and report serious breaches to the Information Commissioner's Office within 72 hours.
- **It wins trust.** Only 12% of small businesses hold Cyber Essentials, according to the same survey. The certificate is something to show clients, and some contracts ask for it.

## Decision needed

1. Approve Phase 1 now (about £1,100 to £2,400).
2. Approve the Microsoft 365 upgrade and Phase 2 budget (about £2,700 to £4,300) for the next 3 months.
3. Name the practice manager as the person responsible, with 2 hours a week set aside.
